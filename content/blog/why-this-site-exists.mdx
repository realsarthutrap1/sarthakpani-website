## Perpetually between states

Most days, my attention feels like a working superposition.

Not in the literal quantum-mechanical sense. I’m borrowing the image.

Several possibilities tend to remain active at once: a product question, a
circuit, a class assignment, a book, a CAD model, or an idea I haven’t found
words for yet. If you were to catch me at any given moment, you would probably
see only one of them, whatever task happens to be in front of me. That
observation is only a snapshot, not a description of the entire complex system.

To be honest, I usually wake up thinking about the product. Those thoughts
compound throughout the day, occasionally reaching critical mass around dinner.
My family has learned that conversing with me can accidentally trigger a
twenty-minute product monologue.

My morning ablutions are where the spark plugs in my brain start to apply
voltage. I brush my teeth in the shower. Yes, I brush in the shower. I usually
zone out long enough for some half-life problem to become slightly less
half-formed.

From there, I sit down in front of three monitors, allowing the possibilities
to collapse into whatever needs doing. Depending on the day, it might mean
working through thirty credits over the summer. It might mean setting up yet
another harness, preparing something for a client, or calling Ben to ramble
through an idea. Occasionally, it means breaking a piece of hardware and
reordering it from Temu.

If you skimmed the sentences above, the TL;DR is simple: my work doesn’t fit
neatly into a single title.

It’s not that I have ADHD, although I know plenty of brilliant inventors who do.
My curiosity simply refuses to stay in one category.

## The graph, not the feed

I have little interest in compressing all of that into a polished professional
identity. Short-form media seems to have scaffolded itself around compression,
and compression usually removes uncertainty first. By the time ideas reach a
professional feed, the mistakes, reversals, and confused middle sections
mysteriously disappear.

LinkedIn is particularly good at this. Someone you bumped into at Safeway three
days ago is suddenly revolutionizing the nuclear-powered Honda Civic. Sh\*t,
maybe they are, but more often than not, I’m calling their bluff.

X gets a partial pass. At least some of its machinery is visible now that it is
open source. I appreciate getting value and still being allowed to look under
the hood.

I think the real point here is that I prefer long-form writing. If you made it
this far, maybe it was worth the read. I would rather preserve some evidence of
the thinking while it is still occurring.

This site is an honest attempt to graph the way I think instead of flattening
my life into a feed.

This blog is the `/src` folder of my mind. It is structured like a series of
interconnected knowledge graphs. The nodes are my projects, essays, reading
notes, experiments, and unfinished ideas. The edges are the connections I
discover between them, sometimes immediately and sometimes years later.

Don’t blame me if some of it reads like total sh*t. I’m iterating. A lot of this
simply marks where my thinking was at a particular moment in time. Ideas may
remain unresolved.

## A commitment to writing

The more pragmatic use for this site is simply becoming a better writer.

I believe curiosity is fragmented. Writing is, in some ways, an attempt to tidy
those fragments. It forces me to examine them, not just ponder how they connect.
Physically, it turns something in my head into an object that can be questioned,
corrected, remembered, and shared.

Some of the inspiration for this website came from two books in my reading log:
[*Guns, Germs, and Steel*](/books) and [*Sapiens*](/books).

In Chapter 12 of *Guns, Germs, and Steel*, Jared Diamond describes writing as
infrastructure for recordkeeping and for transmitting knowledge across time and
distance. As he puts it, writing made transmission “easier, more detailed, more
accurate, and more persuasive.”

In Chapter 7 of *Sapiens*, Yuval Noah Harari approaches the same development
through the limits of human memory. As societies became larger and more
complicated, information could no longer live exclusively inside individual
brains. Writing gave that information somewhere else to exist.

In both accounts, writing does more than preserve thought. It changes the scale
at which thought can be accumulated, organized, and transmitted.

My purpose here is much smaller, and perhaps more selfish, but I think it is
related.

Writing allows an idea to survive beyond the moment in which it was conceived.
This site is where I publish what I think I know. It is also where I preserve
what I am still testing, leaving a record of the discrepancy between the two.

Hopefully, over time, it will help both of us understand how my brain actually
works.
